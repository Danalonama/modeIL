## 1. On-site "Process" section (short, first-person)

**How this got made**

I didn't set out to build a website. I set out to stop losing track.

I'm the person who notices where someone got their shirt. I can spend a whole afternoon in and out of stores, trying things on, falling for a brand I'd never heard of — and then forgetting its name a week later. After enough of those, I started keeping a list. The list got long. The list became this.

ModeIL is, at its heart, a discovery engine. Israel is full of designers doing quietly extraordinary work, and most people will never hear their names. I wanted one place where you could just *browse* — by mood, by style, by neighborhood — and find your next favorite label.

I added every brand by hand, one at a time. Each one got a real photo and a description drawn from the designer's own words, because a directory that flattens everyone into the same template isn't a directory worth making. I made hard calls about who belongs and who doesn't, what's a "designer" versus a "boutique," and how to tag things so they're actually findable.

It's a labour of love, and it will always be free. If you make something in Israel and you're not here yet — talk to me. I'd love to add you.

## 2. Portfolio / case study (product-designer framing)

**ModeIL — designing a discovery engine for Israeli fashion**

*Role: Designer, researcher, builder, curator — solo project*

**The problem**

Israel has a deep bench of independent fashion designers, and almost no good way to discover them. Talent is scattered across hundreds of separate Shopify stores, Instagram accounts, and word-of-mouth. As someone who follows this world closely, I kept "finding" the same brands over and over because there was nowhere to *hold* them. If it was hard for me, it was impossible for everyone else.

**Where it started — a real user, not a hypothetical one**

The project has a clear origin: my friend Anat, who lives in Washington, came to visit. She loves fashion, she wanted to support Israel with where she spends her money, and she asked me a simple question — *who should I be buying from here?* So I sat down and made her a long list of designers.

That list was the first version of ModeIL. And Anat turned out to be a whole persona: people living abroad who love fashion and want their money to go to Israeli makers, but have no idea where to look. They're motivated, they're ready to buy, and the information simply doesn't exist in any usable form. Designing for Anat — not for an abstract "user" — kept the project honest. Every decision came back to: *would this actually help someone like her find, trust, and reach a designer she'd love?*

**The approach**

I treated it as a discovery and curation problem, not a catalog problem.

- **Curation over completeness.** I made deliberate editorial calls — pulling famous heritage houses that didn't fit the spirit of the index, removing brands that turned out not to be Israeli, drawing a clear line between *designers* and *multi-brand boutiques*. The value isn't in listing everything; it's in listing the right things, well.
- **A real information architecture.** Every brand carries structured metadata: a two-axis tag system (Style — edgy, minimalist, legacy… / Type — menswear, jewelry, swimwear…), a region, an "online vs. in-person" status, and a physical address where one exists. That structure powers layered filters, an A–Z, full-text search, and a map of brick-and-mortar stores.
- **Details that respect the work.** Each entry has a hand-chosen image and a description grounded in the designer's own language. Broken image links fall back to a colored name-thumbnail, so the grid never looks broken. The layout is a Pinterest-style masonry that still reads alphabetically left-to-right — staggered and editorial, but never disorienting.
- **An aesthetic with a point of view.** Stark, type-led, editorial. Hard corners, a single confident typeface, lots of breathing room — a frame that gets out of the way and lets the clothes speak.

**How I source — staying genuinely current**

The index doesn't grow from a Google search. I have a real soft spot for edgy, new, fringe brands, so I go looking where they actually live: younger designer events, fairs, pop-ups. I notice the labels on people whose style catches my eye and track them down later. I'm constantly updating. That ongoing, on-the-ground sourcing is why the list skews toward discovery rather than the obvious household names — it's the difference between a directory that's *researched* and one that's genuinely *plugged in*.

**The image curation — an invisible set of rules**

The single image that represents each brand does a huge amount of work, so I select every one against an unwritten rulebook I developed along the way:

- **Typical, not generic.** The image should show pieces that are characteristic of the brand and capture its specific point of view.
- **Local when possible.** If a brand has photos that look shot in Israel, I choose those — I love that the localness shows.
- **Interesting over conventional.** When a brand uses diverse models, I pick the more striking looks, not the default skinny-model shot.
- **Respectful.** No blunt, over-sexualized images that objectify women or men.
- **Careful.** No cropped or cut-off faces, and nothing that could cause distress — this is a country carrying a lot of collective trauma, and the imagery should hold that awareness.
- **Personal.** Ideally a photo featuring pieces I genuinely loved and would want myself.
- **A sensible default.** When I'm unsure, I choose from a brand's best-sellers while still applying every criterion above.

These choices are quiet, but they're a big part of why the grid feels varied, human, and considered rather than like a stock catalog. The curation *is* the design.

**The hard parts**

The interesting problems were rarely visual. They were judgment calls: *Is this brand still active? Is it actually Israeli? Designer or store? Which of its many tags are true?* Multiply that by a few hundred and the project becomes an exercise in consistency and editorial discipline — keeping the taxonomy honest as the dataset grew.

**Where it is now**

A living index of ~270 designers and boutiques, filterable by style, type, and area, with a store map and a clear distinction between online-only and in-person labels. It grows weekly. I'm now carefully exploring affiliate links to cover hosting — structured so it can never influence who's featured or how they're described. Editorial independence is the whole point.

**What I'd carry forward**

Build the structure before you scale the content. The moment I committed to a clean schema — tags, regions, status — every later feature (filters, map, search) became easy. The discipline up front is what let the thing keep growing without turning to mush.

**Full circle**

The best validation didn't come from analytics. A few months after her visit, I sent Anat the finished site. She ordered a t-shirt from ATA — a brand that reminded her of her childhood in Israel. That's the entire project in one purchase: someone far from home, finding a designer she connects with, and supporting Israeli fashion in a way she simply couldn't before. The list I once scribbled for one friend now does that quietly, for anyone who lands on it.

## 3. Blog post / newsletter essay (longer, narrative)

**I made a place for the designers I kept forgetting**

I have a confession that won't surprise anyone who knows me: I love clothes. Not in a casual way. I can stare at fashion sites for hours. I clock where strangers got their jackets. Give me a free afternoon and I'll spend it walking store to store, trying things on, talking to whoever's behind the counter.

And for years, the same thing kept happening. I'd discover some small Israeli label doing genuinely beautiful work — a designer cutting things you couldn't find anywhere else — and then, a few weeks later, I couldn't remember the name. It was gone. The discovery didn't stick because there was nowhere to put it.

So I started a list. Just for me, at first.

The more I added, the more obvious the real problem became. It wasn't *my* memory. It was that all this talent had no shared home. Every designer was on their own island — a Shopify store here, an Instagram there, a studio you could only visit by appointment. Incredible work, almost completely undiscoverable unless you already knew to look. The people who'd love these brands had no way to find them, and the designers had no easy way to be found.

That gap is the whole reason ModeIL exists.

I built it brand by brand, by hand. Every single entry — and there are around 270 now — I added one at a time: a photo I chose, a description in the designer's own voice, the right tags, the neighborhood, whether you can visit in person or only shop online. It's slow, and that's the point. A directory that treats everyone as interchangeable rows isn't worth making. The care is the product.

A lot of the work was invisible — and it was the most important part. Deciding who belongs. Pulling the big heritage names when they didn't fit the spirit of the thing. Quietly removing a brand when I realized it wasn't actually Israeli. Drawing the line between a *designer* and a *boutique that carries designers*. Keeping the tags honest so that when you filter for "minimalist" or "edgy" or "swimwear," you actually get what you asked for. None of that shows on the surface. All of it is why the surface works.

The look is deliberately stark — editorial, type-led, lots of white space, no clutter. I wanted a frame that disappears so the clothes can talk. Underneath that calm surface is a fair amount of fussing: a layout that staggers like a Pinterest board but still reads alphabetically left to right, fallback thumbnails so nothing ever looks broken, filters by style and type and area, a map of the physical stores.

ModeIL is a labour of love, and it stays free. I'm only now, carefully, looking at affiliate links — purely to cover the cost of running it — and I've built that side so it can never touch the editorial choices. Who's listed, how they're described, where they appear: none of that is for sale, and it never will be.

If you like fashion and you like local designers, this is for you. And if you *are* one of those designers and you're not here yet — please, talk to me. Adding you would be my favorite part of the week.
